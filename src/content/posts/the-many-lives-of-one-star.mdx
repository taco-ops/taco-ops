# The Many Lives of One Star

A human life is rarely a single continuous thing. It is revised under pressure, interrupted by luck, illness, work, love, fear, boredom, and the strange accidents that later acquire the dignity of narrative.

Some people remain close for years. Others drift away without villainy or drama. Distance does its work quietly.

# Early Sparks

As a teenager I was restless, always chasing new hobbies. Breakdancing, skateboarding, running. Each one burned hot for a while, then cooled, leaving behind the minor embarrassment familiar to anyone who has mistaken intensity for permanence.

Two stayed with me: guitar and technology.

At 13 my dad gave me a Fender Squier and a small Peavey amp. I played until I fell asleep with the guitar in my hands. Sometimes I woke before sunrise and sat in my closet, strumming softly so I wouldn’t wake anyone.

Later I played in a band. That was the first time I stood in front of people and learned what it meant not to disappear into fear.

At 18 I bought some used computer gear from a business that was closing down. I thought I was buying a PC. Instead, I brought home a Windows NT 2000 server, a deeply unglamorous object that changed the course of my life with no concern for presentation.

I learned to dual boot. I installed Linux Mandrake. I realized that the same curiosity that drove me to music could also drive me to systems, though systems were less likely to complain about timing.

# Drifting Years

My twenties and early thirties were quieter. I let the fire burn low, which is a gentler phrase than the thing itself deserves.

I worked as a graphic designer, even though I had been building websites since 2001. I did not push myself. I drifted, carrying on while mistaking motion for progress.

Then came 2012. A billboard in Houston read: “Do you know Linux? We’re Hiring.” I applied. I got the job.

That moment restarted everything.

I began in front-line support, troubleshooting on chat and phone calls. My old Linux skills became useful again. I moved into training new employees, and step by step I found myself climbing.

# Survival and What Follows

Then came cancer.

The fight was consuming, but remission asked an even harder question: What do you do when you are not dying anymore?

Afterward I moved to Austin, a place I had wanted to call home since the 90s.

At WP Engine I started again, moving from support to NOC, then to TechOps, to Team Lead, and finally Production Engineer. I spent nearly seven years there.

Each role mattered less for its title and more for what it represented. Proof that I was still here. Proof that survival had to become something larger than endurance, or it would curdle into mere waiting.

# What Remains

I no longer believe that “finding yourself” is a destination. Identity is less fixed than we pretend. It shifts, sheds, reignites, and occasionally returns to an older form with better evidence.

There is something steady at the core. For me it has been music, technology, and survival. That is the thread.

I have always hoped I would find myself. The phrase barely survives scrutiny, but gratitude has kept it alive for me.

Because I am still here.
And being here at all means there is another life yet to live.

# A Wider View

We each carry many lives inside us. Some are chosen. Some arrive with paperwork, diagnosis codes, job titles, old songs, and the sudden knowledge that the person you were has no further use for your permission.

I am wary of calling that cosmic. It is human, which is difficult enough.

I am still here. For now, that is plenty.
